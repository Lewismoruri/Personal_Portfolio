I Found 5 Ways Data Analysts Can Make Money Outside a 9–5 Here’s What I Learned

Getting a job in 2026 isn’t as straightforward as many of us hoped it would be. And even when you do have a job, many people are thinking about how to increase their income and create more financial security. So, why limit yourself to one source of income when your skills could potentially solve problems for other people?

I recently came across a YouTube video by Natassha Selvaraj about data analytics side hustles, and it got me thinking about how the skills I’m building could be useful beyond getting a traditional 9–5.

The video explored five different ways data analysts can potentially make money outside of a full-time job. Here are the ideas that stood out to me.

1. Create Content About What You’re Learning

You don’t necessarily have to be an expert to start sharing what you know.

You can write about an SQL concept you just learned, explain an Excel technique, break down a Power BI dashboard, or document how you approached a project.

This one stood out to me because I’ve been learning that you can document your journey while you’re still on it. I don’t have to wait until I know everything before I start sharing.

2. Help Small Businesses With Their Data

Think about the small businesses around us.

A business may not need a full-time data analyst, but they could have months of sales information sitting in Excel. Maybe their records are scattered across different spreadsheets, they don’t know which products are performing best, or they just need a simple dashboard to understand what’s happening in their business.

Those are still data problems. And sometimes, the solution doesn’t need to be complicated.

3. Combine Data Skills With What You Already Know

Another idea was leveraging previous industry or professional experience. Someone with a background in finance who learns data analytics, for example, can combine the two.

That made me think about something I’ve been slowly realizing: Your previous experience doesn’t necessarily become useless when you move into data. It can actually become part of what makes you different.

4. Technical Writing

This was probably the one I hadn’t really considered before.

Data and software companies need people who can explain technical products clearly. That could mean documentation, tutorials, guides, blog posts, or other educational content.

What I found interesting is the idea of demonstrating your ability before asking someone to hire you. Instead of simply saying:

“I’m good at technical writing.”

You could identify something that could be improved, make a useful contribution, and then introduce yourself. That’s a completely different way of looking at opportunities.

5. Build Your Portfolio by Solving Real Problems

This one probably resonated with me the most.

We’re often told to “build projects” when learning data analytics. But there’s a difference between building another practice project from a dataset online and solving a problem that someone actually has.

A small real-world project can give you more than something to add to your CV.

  • It can give you experience.
  • It can give you a portfolio piece.
  • It can give you a testimonial.
  • It can introduce you to someone.
  • And sometimes, it can lead to something bigger.

What Am I Taking Away From All This?

I’m still learning. I’m still building my skills in Excel, SQL, Python, and Power BI. And I’m still looking for opportunities to put those skills to work.

But this video reminded me that getting a job isn’t the only way to create value with what I’m learning.

There are businesses with data problems. There are people who need things explained.

There are companies that need documentation. There are things I can build and share.

And there are problems I can potentially help solve.

I’m not saying I have all of this figured out. Far from it.

But maybe that’s the point of Still Figuring It Out.

I’m learning to look beyond the obvious path.

Learn. Build. Share. Solve problems.

And see where that takes me.
