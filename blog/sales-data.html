Turning Raw Sales Data Into Actionable Business Decisions

A dataset can contain thousands of rows of sales information, but numbers alone don't tell a business what to do. In this project, I used Power BI to explore e-commerce sales data and uncover insights around profitability, regional performance, customer segments, and year-over-year trends.

The Project

This project was part of the Future Interns Data Analytics Track. The goal was to take raw e-commerce sales data and turn it into an interactive dashboard that could help decision-makers understand what was driving business performance.

Rather than simply presenting totals, I wanted the dashboard to answer practical questions:

  • Which products are generating the most profit?
  • Which areas are performing poorly?
  • How is performance changing over time?
  • Which customer segments contribute most to sales?
  • Where might the business need to investigate further?

Starting With the Data

Before building the visuals, I focused on preparing the dataset for analysis. Raw data isn't automatically ready for a dashboard, so cleaning and transformation were important parts of the process.

I prepared the data, structured the model, and created the calculations needed to analyse sales, profit, returns, and trends.

This stage reminded me of something important: a visually impressive dashboard is only as reliable as the data behind it.

Looking Beyond Sales

One of the most interesting findings came from looking at profitability rather than sales alone.

The Technology category stood out, with Copiers emerging as one of the most profitable areas in the dataset.

On the other hand, Tables within the Furniture category were among the most loss-making areas.

This highlighted an important distinction: high sales do not necessarily mean high profit.

Looking at both revenue and profitability gives a business a much clearer understanding of which products are actually contributing to its bottom line.

Where Is the Business Performing Best?

I also analysed performance geographically to understand how results differed across states.

California emerged as the strongest performing state, while Texas was among the weaker performers based on profit.

The geographic view made these differences easier to spot. Instead of looking through rows of state-level numbers, the map provided a quick visual overview of where performance was strongest and where further investigation might be needed.

Importantly, the dashboard doesn't explain why California performs better than Texas. It does something more useful at this stage: it tells us where to start asking better questions.

Are We Actually Improving?

Looking at a single year's performance only tells part of the story. To understand whether the business was improving, I included year-over-year comparisons.

The dashboard compares:

  • Sales
  • Profit
  • Percentage of returned orders

The sales trend also compares the current year with the previous year, making it easier to identify changes in performance over time.

One detail I found particularly interesting was the treatment of returned orders. A decrease in the percentage of returned orders is actually a positive outcome, even though the percentage change itself is negative.

That was a useful reminder that numbers need context before they can become insights.

Understanding the Customers

Sales were also broken down into three customer segments: Consumer, Corporate, and Home Office.

Segmenting customers provides another layer of context. Instead of treating every customer as part of one large group, the business can compare how different customer types contribute to overall sales.

This opens the door to deeper questions around customer behaviour, profitability, and which segments might deserve more attention.

Making the Insights Easy to See

Analysis is only useful if people can understand it. That's where visualization and dashboard design become important.

I used different visuals for different questions: geographic maps for regional performance, trend lines for year-over-year analysis, and segment visuals for understanding customer contributions.

I also used conditional formatting to make performance differences easier to identify. Profitability extremes were visually highlighted so that strong and weak performers could be recognised quickly.

The goal wasn't to fill the dashboard with charts. It was to make the important information easier to find.

What I Learned From the Project

The biggest lesson I took from this project wasn't a particular Power BI feature.

It was learning to think beyond the numbers.

A good analyst doesn't stop at: "What happened?"

The next questions are: "Why did it happen?" and "What should we investigate or do next?"

Building this dashboard gave me an opportunity to practise that process—from cleaning and modelling data, to creating DAX calculations, analysing trends, and communicating findings visually.

From Data to Decisions

The journey from raw data to a useful business insight can be summarised simply:

Raw Data → Clean Data → Analysis → Visualization → Insight → Decision

But the real work happens in between those steps. It requires curiosity, careful analysis, and the ability to connect numbers to real business questions.

That's what I enjoy about working with data. It's not just about finding numbers.

It's about finding the story behind them—and making that story useful.
