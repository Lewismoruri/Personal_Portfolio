I Analysed Netflix's Movies and TV Shows

When I think about Netflix, I don't immediately think about spreadsheets, PivotTables or charts. I think about the endless list of movies and TV shows that appears whenever I open the platform and the familiar question of what to watch next.

Looking Behind the Library

Behind that seemingly endless library is a lot of data.

For this project, I wanted to look beyond the Netflix interface and explore that data myself. I wanted to understand how the library changed over time, whether Netflix was more heavily focused on movies or TV shows, which countries contributed the most titles, and what the ratings of those titles looked like.

I used the Netflix Titles dataset from Kaggle and built an interactive dashboard in Excel to tell that story.

Starting With the Data

The dataset contains information about Netflix movies and TV shows, including their titles, release years, countries, ratings, and whether they are classified as a Movie or TV Show.

Before building any charts, however, I had to deal with the data itself. As with most real-world datasets, it wasn't simply a case of opening the file and immediately creating a dashboard. I cleaned and prepared the data so that it could be used consistently for the different parts of my analysis.

This also meant being careful about how I interpreted the numbers. Some records were excluded during the cleaning process depending on the analysis being performed, so the totals shown in different parts of the dashboard do not necessarily add up directly. Rather than forcing the numbers to reconcile, I treated each analysis according to the cleaned data relevant to it.

That process was an important part of the project for me. It reminded me that the quality of a dashboard depends heavily on what happens before the first chart is created.

So, What Happened to Netflix's Library?

The first thing I wanted to understand was how the number of movies and TV shows released changed over time. And this is where the story became interesting.

The number of releases increased considerably throughout the 2010s. The growth becomes particularly noticeable toward the latter part of the decade, with Netflix reaching its highest levels of releases around 2018 and 2019.

At first glance, the trend might simply look like Netflix was producing more and more content every year. But then the line changes direction. Around 2020, the number of releases begins to fall, and the decline continues into 2021.

This period is important because it coincides with the COVID-19 pandemic. Film and television production around the world was heavily disrupted as productions were paused, filming schedules were delayed and releases were pushed back.

So the decline in Netflix releases during this period shouldn't simply be interpreted as Netflix losing momentum. The wider entertainment industry was dealing with an extraordinary disruption to its normal production and release cycles. Looking at the data in that context makes the trend much more meaningful.

Movies Still Dominate

Another question I wanted to answer was whether Netflix's library was primarily made up of movies or TV shows. The answer was fairly clear.

Movies account for roughly 71% of the analyzed titles, while TV shows make up about 29%. That doesn't necessarily mean that movies are more important to Netflix than TV shows. It simply tells us that, within the dataset I analyzed, movies make up the larger share of the library.

It was one of those findings that seems simple once you see it, but becomes much easier to understand when represented visually.

Netflix Is Global, but the United States Stands Out

The next part of the analysis looked at countries associated with the titles in the dataset. The United States stands out significantly, contributing more titles than any other country in the analysis. India, the United Kingdom, Japan and several other countries also feature prominently.

This was one of the aspects of the dataset that I found most interesting because it shows how Netflix's library reflects a combination of global production and distribution.

Netflix may be a global platform, but the composition of its library is not evenly distributed across countries. There are clear differences in how strongly individual countries are represented.

What About Ratings?

I also looked at the ratings assigned to the movies and TV shows. The distribution shows a strong presence of mature-rated titles, particularly categories such as TV-MA, TV-14 and R.

This provides another perspective on the library. Looking only at the number of movies and TV shows tells us about quantity, but looking at ratings gives us an idea of the types of audiences represented within that library.

Turning the Analysis Into a Dashboard

Once I had explored the data, the next challenge was figuring out how to communicate all of this without overwhelming the person looking at it. I built the final dashboard in Excel using PivotTables, PivotCharts and a year slicer covering 2010 to 2021.

I wanted the dashboard to do more than display charts. I wanted someone to be able to look at it and understand the story fairly quickly: Netflix's library expanded significantly through the late 2010s, movies make up the majority of the analyzed titles, the United States is a major contributor to the library, and the sharp decline in releases around 2020–2021 occurred during a period when COVID-19 disrupted the global film and television industry.

The year slicer also allows the viewer to explore how the different numbers and charts change across the period rather than looking at the data as one static snapshot.

What This Project Taught Me

The biggest lesson for me wasn't actually about Netflix. It was about the process of working with data.

There is a tendency to think that data analysis begins when you create a chart. In reality, there is a lot that happens before that point: understanding the dataset, cleaning it, deciding which records can be used, asking the right questions, and figuring out which visualizations actually help answer those questions.

I also learned that a dashboard doesn't need to show everything you've discovered. The underlying dataset contains far more information than what ended up on the final dashboard. The challenge was deciding what mattered to the story and what would only create noise.

For me, that's what made this project worthwhile. I started with a dataset of Netflix titles, but ended up with a much better understanding of how to turn raw data into something that another person can actually explore and understand.

Project Details

  • Dataset: Netflix Titles Dataset, Kaggle
  • Tool: Microsoft Excel
  • Techniques: Data cleaning, PivotTables, PivotCharts, interactive slicers and dashboard design
  • Period analyzed: 2010–2021
  • Dashboard: Netflix Movies & TV Shows Analysis
